## Why Menorca Really Does Need a Car

Menorca is smaller than Mallorca and quieter than Ibiza, but it's not a place you can see on foot or by taxi. The island is roughly forty kilometres end to end, with a single main road, the Me-1, running like a spine between Mahón in the east and Ciutadella in the west. Almost everything worth seeing, the calm southern coves, the rugged north coast, the prehistoric talayotic sites scattered across farmland, sits off that spine down minor roads that public transport simply doesn't cover well. If you're staying anywhere outside the main resorts, or you want to reach beaches like Macarelleta, Cala Turqueta or Binimel·là before the day-trip crowds arrive, a car isn't a luxury. It's the only practical way to do it.

## The Road From Mahón to Cala Macarelleta

From the airport, the drive towards Menorca's famous southwestern coves takes around forty-five minutes to an hour, mostly along the Me-1 dual carriageway before you turn off onto narrower country roads near Ciutadella. Cala Macarelleta, the smaller neighbour of the better-known Cala Macarella, has no direct car park at the beach itself. Most drivers leave the car at the main car park for Cala Macarella and walk the coastal path a short distance to reach Macarelleta, a walk that's easy but worth doing early in the day before the midday sun and the crowds build up. The roads getting there are well surfaced but narrow in places, with occasional traffic from other holidaymakers heading the same direction, so a smaller or mid-sized car generally makes the last stretch easier than a large SUV.

## Exploring the Camí de Cavalls by Car and Foot

The Camí de Cavalls is Menorca's historic coastal path, circling the entire island and originally used for patrolling the coastline on horseback. You can't drive the path itself, much of it is walking or cycling only, but a car is essential for reaching the access points scattered around the island's edge. Different sections start from small car parks near villages like Es Grau, Fornells or Cala Galdana, and having your own car means you can pick off short, spectacular stretches of the path without committing to a full-day trek. This flexibility, drive to a trailhead, walk an hour or two, drive to the next one, is exactly the kind of itinerary that only works with a car of your own rather than relying on limited local buses.

## The Wild North Coast

Menorca's north coast is a different island altogether from the calm southern coves. Beaches like Binimel·là and Cavalleria are more exposed to wind and swell, backed by dramatic cliffs and open farmland rather than pine woods. The roads up here are quieter but can be rougher, with some final approaches to beaches running along unsurfaced tracks. It's worth checking with your car hire provider which vehicle handles these stretches most comfortably, and driving a little more slowly than you would on the Me-1. The reward is beaches that feel genuinely remote, often with only a handful of other cars in the car park even in high season.

## Driving Notes for UK Visitors

Spain drives on the right, which takes most UK drivers a short adjustment period, particularly at roundabouts and when pulling out of car parks. A full, valid UK driving licence is generally all that's needed for a hire car in Spain, though it's worth checking current requirements before you travel. Speed limits are posted in kilometres per hour, and many rural Menorcan roads are narrower than UK equivalents, so a little extra care on blind bends near stone walls is sensible. Fuel stations are less frequent once you're off the Me-1, so it's worth planning ahead on longer north coast excursions.
