## Landing at Gran Canaria Airport (LPA): What Arrival Actually Looks Like

Gran Canaria Airport, often referred to locally as Gando, has a single main terminal handling both domestic and international arrivals, including the regular UK routes from London Gatwick, Manchester, Bristol, Birmingham and other regional airports. Once you're through passport control and baggage reclaim, you'll come out into the main arrivals hall, where the traditional rental car desks are lined up along one side, usually with a queue forming as soon as any wide-bodied or charter flight lands.

With meet and greet car hire, you skip that entirely. Instead of joining the queue, you head straight for the arranged meeting point, typically just outside arrivals or in the short-term pick-up zone, where your driver is waiting with the car. There's no separate shuttle bus to an off-site depot and no second queue once you get there, which matters at an airport where depot transfers can easily add another thirty minutes to your journey after a long flight.

## Why This Matters More on the First Day of a Spanish Holiday

Gran Canaria is an island where the driving is on the right, opposite to what UK drivers are used to, and most hire cars here are left-hand drive. Getting behind the wheel of an unfamiliar car, on an unfamiliar side of the road, after being awake since a 5am airport transfer at home, is not the moment to also be filling out desk paperwork or working out where a depot shuttle stops. Having the car delivered to you at the airport means you get a few calmer minutes to adjust to the vehicle, check the controls and get your bearings before joining traffic.

## Roads and Routes UK Drivers Should Know on Gran Canaria

From the airport, the GC-1 motorway runs south towards the main UK holiday resorts of Maspalomas, Playa del Inglés and San Agustín, a drive of roughly 35 to 40 minutes depending on traffic. Heading north on the GC-2 takes you towards Las Palmas de Gran Canaria, the island's capital, in around 20 to 25 minutes. Both roads are well-surfaced dual carriageways, easy for anyone used to UK motorways, though speed limits are in kilometres per hour and tolls are not typically charged on this island.

Venture inland towards Tejeda, Roque Nublo or the mountain villages, and the roads become narrower and more winding, with hairpin bends climbing towards the island's volcanic centre. These routes reward a bit of local knowledge and a car you're already comfortable driving, which is another reason many UK visitors prefer to get the unfamiliarity of the vehicle out of the way at the airport rather than partway up a mountain road.
